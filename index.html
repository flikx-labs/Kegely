How to use it

  1. Pick a level, or set your own timings in Custom.
  2. On “Squeeze,” gently lift and tighten while breathing normally. The circle shrinks with you.
  3. On “Relax,” let go fully. The circle grows back.
  4. Slow squeezes build endurance. The quick squeezes at the end train fast reactions, like for a cough or sneeze.
  5. Keep it comfortable and steady rather than as hard as possible.

What is the pelvic floor?

It runs from your pubic bone to your tailbone and supports the bladder and bowel, and the prostate. In men these muscles help you hold and empty urine, control wind, and play a part in erections and ejaculation. They also work with your deep core. Stronger, better-timed muscles can help with dribbling and leaks, may help some men with erections, and are commonly used to recover after prostate surgery.

Possible benefits

  • Bladder control. Stronger muscles can reduce dribbling and leaks, including dribbling after you finish urinating. This is among the best-supported benefits.
  • Recovery after prostate surgery. Training before and after is commonly used to help regain urinary control sooner.
  • Firmer erections. The muscles at the base of the penis help keep an erection firm. Studies suggest training may help some men with erection difficulties.
  • Ejaculation control. Better awareness and timing of these muscles may help some men last longer. Studies are small and results vary.
  • Bowel control. The same muscles help hold in wind and stool.
  • Core and pelvic support. They work with your deep core, abdominals and back to support the pelvis.

Evidence is strongest for bladder control, and weaker (small, varied studies) for erections and ejaculation. Results aren’t guaranteed and usually take weeks to months of regular practice. Kegels don’t replace treatment: if erection or ejaculation problems continue, see a doctor.

Positions

Start lying down, where gravity helps. Move up a position once the squeeze feels easy and controlled.

Find the right muscles

  • Sit or lie down and breathe normally. Imagine holding back wind, then also stopping the flow of urine. You should feel a gentle squeeze and lift inside.
  • In a mirror you may see the base of the penis draw in and the testicles lift slightly. Your belly, buttocks and thighs stay soft.
  • Keep your buttocks, thighs and stomach soft. If they tighten, ease off.
  • Stopping urine mid-flow is only an occasional check, not an exercise.

Common mistakes

  • Holding your breath
  • Squeezing buttocks, thighs or abs instead
  • Not relaxing fully between squeezes
  • Squeezing as hard as possible until you tire

Build up gradually

  • Start with holds you can do cleanly, then add seconds and reps. A common goal is 10 holds of up to 10 seconds plus 10 quick squeezes, about 3 times a day.
  • Begin lying down, then sitting, then standing.
  • Tie sessions to a habit, like after brushing your teeth or when the kettle’s on.
  • Expect change over weeks to months. Many programmes run for at least 3 months.

Routines follow the pattern in NHS physiotherapy leaflets (slow holds, then quick squeezes, about 3 times a day) and NICE’s advice to train for at least 3 months.

The knack

Tighten your pelvic floor just before you cough, sneeze, laugh or lift, and hold it until you’re done. It helps protect against leaks when pressure rises.

Check with a professional first if

  • You have pelvic pain, pain during sex, or trouble relaxing or starting to pass urine. Strengthening can make a tight pelvic floor worse, and relaxing may need to come first.
  • You’ve had prostate or pelvic surgery, or have a prostate condition. Follow your clinician’s plan.

Stay safe

Stop if you feel pain or your symptoms get worse. Avoid holding your breath or tensing your thighs, buttocks or stomach instead. If you’re unsure Kegels are right for you, ask a doctor or pelvic floor physiotherapist. This is educational and not medical advice.
